# A Later Start Is Not the Easy Fix It Seems

*By Daniel Okafor. An opinion article written for this unit; the writer is fictional, the facts are real.*

Nobody wants exhausted students. Teenagers need 8 to 10 hours of sleep a night, and many get far less. But moving the school day later is not the simple answer it sounds like. For many families and communities, a later start would create new problems that are just as serious as the one it tries to solve.

The first problem is transportation. Many school districts do not have enough buses and drivers to take every student to school at the same time. Instead, the same buses make several trips each morning: one for the high school, then the middle school, then the elementary schools. If the high school starts later, something else has to change. The district must buy more buses and hire more drivers, which costs money that could pay for teachers or books, or the younger children must go to school earlier. Many people think it makes little sense to have seven-year-olds waiting at bus stops in the dark.

The second problem is what happens after school. A later start means a later end. If school lets out at 4:00 p.m. instead of 3:00 p.m., sports teams have less daylight for practice, especially in the fall and winter. Games against schools with different schedules become harder to plan. Students who work part-time jobs to help their families would earn less. Others who care for younger brothers and sisters after school might not get home in time.

The third problem is family schedules. Many parents leave for work early. Right now, some can drop their teenagers off at school on the way. With a later start, those students might need to find another way to get there, or stay home alone in the morning. The change would affect far more people than just the students.

Supporters of later start times point to research. In Seattle, for example, high schools moved their start time almost an hour later in 2016, and researchers found that students there slept about half an hour more on school nights. That is a real result. But it comes from one city with its own buses, budgets and families. A change that worked in Seattle might not work the same way somewhere else.

There are also other ways to help students sleep. Schools can teach students about sleep and why it matters. Families can set limits on phones and screens at night, since the light from screens can make it harder to fall asleep. Teachers can make sure homework does not keep students up past midnight. These steps cost little and do not require changing the schedule for an entire community.

Students' sleep matters. But before our school changes its start time, we should look honestly at the costs to families, younger children and the whole community. A good solution should not create as many problems as it solves.
