# Let Us Sleep: Why School Should Start Later

*By Maya Torres. An opinion article written for this unit; the writer is fictional, the facts are real.*

Every school morning, millions of teenagers drag themselves out of bed before the sun is up. Many of them are not lazy. They are tired, and science can explain why. Our school should start at 8:30 a.m. or later, because later start times help students sleep enough, learn better and stay healthier.

First, teenagers need more sleep than most of them get. Sleep experts at the American Academy of Sleep Medicine say that people from 13 to 18 years old need 8 to 10 hours of sleep every night. A student who must catch a bus at 6:45 a.m. would have to fall asleep by about 9:30 p.m. to get even eight hours. For most teenagers, that is not realistic.

The reason is biology, not bad habits. During puberty, the body's internal clock shifts later. The brain starts releasing melatonin, the hormone that makes us sleepy, later in the evening than it did in childhood. That is why many teenagers do not feel tired until around 11 p.m. Asking them to fall asleep earlier is like asking an adult to fall asleep at 7 p.m.

Doctors agree that schools should adjust. In 2014, the American Academy of Pediatrics, an organization of tens of thousands of children's doctors, recommended that middle and high schools start at 8:30 a.m. or later. Yet a report from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention found that fewer than one in five American middle and high schools started that late.

Second, when schools do start later, students really do sleep more. In 2016, the public high schools in Seattle moved their start time from 7:50 a.m. to 8:45 a.m. Scientists from the University of Washington measured students' sleep before and after the change. They found that students slept a little more than half an hour longer on school nights. They did not simply stay up later. In the biology classes the researchers studied, grades went up, and at one school, fewer students were late or absent from first period.

Third, sleep is about more than grades. Tired students have a harder time paying attention, controlling their moods and making safe choices. Teenagers who drive to school are safer behind the wheel when they are rested. Sleep also helps the body fight illness and helps young athletes recover.

Some people say a later start would cause problems with buses, sports and after-school jobs. Those problems are real, but they can be solved. Districts can change bus routes, and teams can adjust practice times. California has already done it: a state law passed in 2019 requires most public high schools there to start no earlier than 8:30 a.m. and most middle schools no earlier than 8:00 a.m. If a whole state can make the change, our school can too.

We expect students to show up ready to learn. The least we can do is give them a schedule that lets them get the sleep their bodies need. It is time to let us sleep.
